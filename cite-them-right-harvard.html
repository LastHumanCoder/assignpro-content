Cite Them Right Harvard Explained

The referencing standard most UK universities actually mean when they say "Harvard", set out with worked examples you can copy the shape of.

Cite Them Right Harvard is the version of Harvard referencing published in the guide Cite Them Right by Richard Pears and Graham Shields, and it is the version most UK universities adopt as their house standard. Harvard is not a single, governed style. It is a family of author and date systems, and individual institutions have historically each published their own variation. Cite Them Right Harvard is the attempt to standardise that family, which is why your reading list, your library guide and your submission portal all point at the same book.

In practice that means two things. In the body of your work you cite by surname and year. At the end you give a single alphabetical reference list with the full details of everything you cited. Everything else on this page is detail hanging off those two rules.

If you are searching for cite them right harvard in your first term, you have probably already noticed the problem: every guide you open shows a slightly different comma. This piece sets out the version UK markers expect, shows harvard referencing examples for the sources students actually use, and is written for harvard referencing for beginners rather than for librarians. One caveat before the examples, and it matters. Your department can and sometimes does publish its own small variations on cite them right harvard. Where your module handbook disagrees with anything below, the handbook wins. Print it, keep it next to you, and apply it consistently. Consistency is what is actually being assessed.

What a Harvard reference is made of

Every reference is the same set of elements in the same order. Once you can see the elements, unfamiliar sources stop being frightening, because you are only ever asking which elements exist for this particular thing.

Read left to right: who made it, when, what it is called, which version, where it was published and by whom. A journal article swaps the last two elements for the journal title, volume, issue and page range. A web page swaps them for an availability statement. The spine of the reference never changes.

Book

Pears, R. and Shields, G. (2022) Cite them right: the essential referencing guide. 12th edn. London: Bloomsbury Academic.

Journal article

Nicol, D. (2010) 'From monologue to dialogue: improving written feedback processes in mass higher education', Assessment and Evaluation in Higher Education, 35(5), pp. 501-517.

Web page

Office for National Statistics (2024) Labour market overview. Available at: https://www.ons.gov.uk/employmentandlabourmarket (Accessed: 3 October 2026).

Three details in those examples are worth pausing on, because they are the ones we correct most often. Titles of whole works go in italics and titles of parts, such as an article inside a journal, go in single quotation marks. The word "edn." is used for edition, not "ed.", which is reserved for editor. And an online source needs both the availability statement and the date you accessed it, because web pages change.

In text citation examples in Harvard

The in text citation is the short pointer in your sentence. It carries the surname and the year, and nothing else unless you are quoting. These in text citation examples harvard markers expect cover almost everything you will meet in a first year.

There is a second choice to make each time, and it is a writing choice rather than a referencing one. You can put the citation inside the brackets, or you can make the author the subject of your sentence:

  • Feedback is more useful when students act on it (Nicol, 2010).
  • Nicol (2010) argues that feedback is more useful when students act on it.

The second form is stronger in critical writing, because it puts a named scholar into your argument rather than parking them in a bracket. Marking rubrics that ask for critical engagement are usually asking, in part, for more of the second form. If your feedback has said your work is descriptive, this is one of the practical levers you can pull, and we go through it in detail in our guidance on being too descriptive and not critical enough.

How to use et al in Harvard referencing

This is the single most asked question we get on this style, so here it is plainly. In the body of your work, Cite Them Right Harvard uses "et al." when a source has four or more authors. With one, two or three authors you name all of them. In the reference list at the end, you give every author's name rather than shortening.

Three mechanical points. "Et al." is an abbreviation of a Latin phrase, so it takes a full stop after "al" and none after "et". It is not italicised in Cite Them Right. And the rule is about how many authors the source has, not about how many times you have cited it, which is a confusion we see constantly.

Reference list or bibliography?

These are not synonyms, and using the wrong one is a quiet way to lose marks. A reference list contains everything you cited in the text, and nothing else. A bibliography contains everything you read, whether you cited it or not. Unless your handbook explicitly asks for a bibliography, a UK Harvard submission wants a reference list.

Both are arranged alphabetically by author surname, both are unnumbered, and neither is split into sections by source type. If two works share an author and a year, you distinguish them with lowercase letters, so 2024a and 2024b, ordered by title, and you use the same letters in your in text citations. The five errors that cost marks

From the work we review, these account for most referencing deductions, and every one of them is mechanical rather than intellectual.

  1. A citation in the text with no matching entry in the list. Markers check this. Work through your final draft and tick each citation against the list before you submit.
  2. Quoting without a page number. Any direct quotation needs a locator. Without one the marker cannot verify it, and in a plagiarism review that absence is what gets queried.
  3. Citing a source you have not read. If you found Kolb quoted inside Pears, you cite it as Kolb cited in Pears and you reference Pears, because Pears is what you actually read.
  4. Inconsistent formatting. Italics on some titles and not others, or the year sometimes bracketed and sometimes not. A reference manager helps here, but only if you check its output, because managers import bad metadata cheerfully.
  5. Undated web sources given a made up year. If no date is shown, the convention is "no date", written out. Do not substitute the year you happened to visit.

The fifth one has a companion problem worth naming. Reference managers and AI tools both produce references that look immaculate and are sometimes for sources that do not exist. Every reference you submit should be one you can open. If a tool generated it, verify it against the actual source before it goes anywhere near your submission, and read our guidance on what an AI flag actually means if you want to understand how this is reviewed.

Where Cite Them Right sits among the others

Harvard is one of several systems you will meet, and which one you use is decided by your department rather than by you. Business and social science modules commonly use Cite Them Right Harvard. Psychology tends to use APA. Law in the UK uses OSCOLA, which is a footnote system and works quite differently. Nursing and health frequently use Vancouver, which numbers sources in the order they appear.

The useful thing to understand is that these are not competing attempts at the same job. Author and date systems keep the evidence visible in the sentence. Numeric and footnote systems keep the line of the prose clean and move the detail below. Neither is better. They suit different disciplines, and your marker is assessing whether you applied the right one properly.

A ten minute referencing routine

Do this at the end of every assignment and referencing stops being a panic.

  1. Sort the reference list alphabetically by surname and read down the left edge to catch duplicates.
  2. Search your document for every opening bracket and confirm each citation has a matching entry.
  3. Check that every direct quotation carries a page number.
  4. Open three references at random from the list and confirm they exist as described.
  5. Compare one entry of each source type against your handbook, not against memory.

That routine catches the five errors above in the order they are most likely to appear. Students who adopt it stop losing the mechanical marks within about two assignments, which is the point at which referencing becomes a habit rather than a task.

Frequently asked questions

What is Cite Them Right Harvard?

It is the version of Harvard referencing set out in the guide Cite Them Right by Pears and Shields, adopted by most UK universities as their house standard. Harvard is a family of author and date styles rather than one governed system, and Cite Them Right is the standardised version UK departments point students to.

How do you use et al in Harvard referencing?

Use et al. in the body of your work when a source has four or more authors, giving the first author's surname followed by et al. and the year. With one, two or three authors, name all of them. In the reference list at the end, give every author's name in full rather than shortening.

Do I need a page number in a Harvard citation?

Always for a direct quotation, written as (Author, Year, p. 23). For paraphrase a page number is optional but helpful, and some departments require it. Missing page numbers on quotations is one of the most common reasons marks are deducted for referencing.

What is the difference between a reference list and a bibliography?

A reference list contains only the sources you cited in the text. A bibliography contains everything you read, cited or not. Unless your handbook asks for a bibliography, a Harvard submission in the UK wants a reference list, arranged alphabetically by author surname.

What do I do if a website shows no date?

Write "no date" in place of the year, in both the citation and the reference list, so (Author, no date). Do not substitute the year you accessed the page and do not abbreviate to n.d. in Cite Them Right. The access date still goes at the end of the reference.
