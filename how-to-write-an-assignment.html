Writing an Assignment, Step by Step

Five stages, in order, with most of the marks decided before you write a sentence.

The reliable way to write an assignment is to decode the brief, plan in claims, read to that plan, draft quickly, then revise against the marking criteria. Most students invert this, reading broadly first and planning afterwards, which is why they run out of time with a full notebook and no argument.

The highest value ten minutes you will spend is on the brief itself. Underline the instruction verb, the scope, the weighting and the criteria. Almost every assignment that misses the point misses it here, not in the writing.

Nobody is taught how to write an assignment at university. You are taught a subject and then assessed by a method the department assumes you already know. This guide sets out the sequence that works, from how to start an assignment when you are staring at the brief, through to the revision pass that recovers the most marks for the least effort.

The five stages

1. Decode the brief

Take the brief and mark four things.

  • The instruction verb. "Evaluate" is not "describe", and "critically discuss" is not "explain". The verb tells you what cognitive work is being assessed.
  • The scope. How many cases, which period, which context. A brief asking for "a UK organisation" is not asking for three.
  • The weighting. If the criteria give 40 per cent to analysis and 10 per cent to context, your word count should reflect that and usually does not.
  • The criteria themselves. These are the marks. Read them as a checklist you will return to at the end.

Common verbs, decoded: describe means say what it is; explain means say how or why it works; analyse means break it into parts and show how they relate; evaluate means judge against criteria and state which is better; critically discuss means weigh competing positions and arrive at one.

2. Plan in claims

Write one sentence per paragraph stating what that paragraph will establish. Not the topic, the claim. Then read the list on its own. If the sequence does not answer the verb, you have found the problem before writing 2,000 words into it.

This is the step students skip, and it is the one that most reliably raises a mark. A plan of claims can be reviewed in five minutes by a tutor, a friend or you the next morning. A finished essay cannot be restructured in five minutes.

3. Read to the plan

Now read, with specific questions in hand. "What evidence supports claim three?" is a searchable question. "Read about motivation" is not, and it is how people lose a fortnight.

Take notes against the claims rather than against the sources. You want a note that says "claim 3: Herzberg found hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate (Herzberg, 1959, p. 113)", not three pages summarising a chapter.

4. Draft fast and badly

Write the whole thing without stopping to polish. A complete rough draft is worth far more than a beautiful half. Editing a bad complete draft is straightforward; finishing a perfect half draft under time pressure is not.

Leave gaps where you need a source, marked clearly, and keep moving. The aim of this stage is to get the argument out of your head and onto the page where it can be assessed.

5. Revise against the criteria

Open the marking criteria beside your draft and work through them one at a time, asking where in the document each is satisfied. If you cannot point to it, the marker will not find it either.

How to write an introduction for an assignment

Write it last, and keep it to about 10 per cent of the word count.

The element most often missing is your position. An introduction that says what the assignment will cover, without saying what it will argue, reads as a contents page. Compare "This essay will discuss motivation theories" with "This essay argues that content theories of motivation explain dissatisfaction better than they explain performance". The second tells a marker there is an argument coming.

Stuck at the planning stage? Send the brief and your plan of claims through the academic guidance form. A subject tutor will tell you whether the plan answers the verb before you spend a week writing to it.

How to start an assignment when you cannot start

The blank page is almost always a planning problem wearing an emotional costume. Three practical moves:

  1. Write the conclusion first. One paragraph stating what you think the answer is. It will change, and it gives everything else a direction.
  2. Start in the middle. There is no rule that says you write in order. Begin with the section you understand best.
  3. Set a twenty minute limit and write badly on purpose. The goal is to produce text that can be improved, not text that is good.

If the block is really about not understanding the material, none of those will work and you should get the concept explained rather than push harder at the page. That is a different problem with a different fix.

The revision pass that recovers the most marks

  • Read only your topic sentences. Do they form a coherent argument on their own? If not, the structure needs work, not the prose.
  • Find every claim without evidence. Search for sentences making assertions and check each has a citation or a reason.
  • Find every piece of evidence without a claim. Quotations and statistics sitting in paragraphs unexplained are the commonest cause of descriptive feedback.
  • Check the verb. If the brief said evaluate, does your work actually judge, or only describe?
  • Check the references. Every citation matched in the list, every quotation with a page number.

The third item is where descriptive writing comes from. Evidence with no interpretation attached reads as reporting, and the fix is to add the sentence that says what it means. Our guidance on being too descriptive and not critical enough works through it in detail.

Time allocation that reflects the marks

For a 2,500 word assignment over three weeks, a realistic allocation is two hours decoding and planning, eight hours reading and note taking against the plan, six hours drafting, and four hours revising. Most students spend twelve hours reading and two revising, which is the wrong way round.

The revision hours are the most productive in the whole process, because they act on a complete draft against known criteria. Protect them by finishing the draft earlier than feels comfortable.

Frequently asked questions

How do you write an assignment step by step?

Decode the brief for verb, scope, weighting and criteria; plan one claim per paragraph; read to that plan with specific questions; draft the whole thing quickly without polishing; then revise against the marking criteria one at a time.

How do you start an assignment when you are stuck?

Write the conclusion first as a single paragraph, or start with the section you understand best rather than the introduction. Setting a twenty minute limit and writing badly on purpose usually breaks the block, because the aim is text that can be improved.

How do you write an introduction for an assignment?

Write it last, at about 10 per cent of the word count, covering context, the problem, your position, the scope and the structure. The element most often missing is the position, which is what tells a marker an argument is coming.

How much time should an assignment take?

For 2,500 words, roughly two hours planning, eight reading, six drafting and four revising. Most students over invest in reading and under invest in revision, which is the most productive stage because it acts on a complete draft against known criteria.

What does the instruction verb in a brief mean?

Describe means say what it is, explain means say how or why, analyse means break it into parts and relate them, evaluate means judge against criteria, and critically discuss means weigh competing positions and reach one. The verb tells you what is being assessed.
