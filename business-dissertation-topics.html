Fifty Business Dissertation Topics

Grouped by specialism, with the data source named for each, because access is what decides whether you finish.

The best business dissertation topics are ones where you already know how you will get the data. Interest comes second. A student with access to one organisation and a modest question will finish. A student with a fascinating question and no route to respondents will not.

Before you commit to any title below, answer one question: who will give me the data, and will they still be willing in four months? If the answer involves cold emailing executives you have never met, choose differently.

Most lists of business dissertation topics give you titles and nothing else, which is why students pick something appealing in October and change it in January. This list names the likely data source and the practical difficulty alongside each area, so you can choose with your eyes open. We supervise a lot of business dissertation work, and the ones that run into trouble almost always fail on access rather than on ideas.

Choose the specialism by data, not by interest

Business is unusually varied in how hard the evidence is to obtain. A marketing survey can be run from a laptop. An operations case study requires an organisation to let you in.

Two patterns worth noticing. Marketing and HR sit at the low difficulty end because survey and questionnaire designs reach respondents who are easy to find, often other students or the general public. Finance and economics look harder but are frequently the easiest of all if you are comfortable with numbers, because the data is public and already collected.

Fifty business dissertation ideas

Marketing

  • Influencer credibility and purchase intention among UK Gen Z consumers.
  • Whether sustainability claims change buying behaviour or only attitudes.
  • User generated content and trust in online reviews.
  • Personalisation and the point at which it becomes intrusive.
  • Loyalty schemes and whether they create loyalty or merely record it.
  • Brand response to public criticism on social platforms.
  • Price framing and perceived value in subscription services.
  • Cause related marketing and consumer scepticism.
  • Packaging design and perceptions of product quality.
  • Whether short form video advertising converts differently by category.

Human resources

  • Hybrid working policy and voluntary turnover in professional services.
  • Psychological safety and whether employees actually report concerns.
  • Onboarding quality and first year retention.
  • The four day week and measured productivity.
  • Line manager behaviour as a predictor of engagement.
  • Whether diversity training changes behaviour or only awareness.
  • Internal mobility and its effect on retention of early career staff.
  • Performance review formats and perceived fairness.
  • Wellbeing provision and whether take up matches need.
  • Employer branding and applicant quality.

Finance and accounting

  • Share price reaction to profit warnings in the FTSE 250.
  • Whether ESG ratings predict financial performance.
  • Audit tenure and the quality of reported earnings.
  • Dividend policy signalling in mature sectors.
  • The cost of capital and green bond issuance.
  • Working capital management in SMEs during rate rises.
  • Whether IFRS 16 changed reported leverage meaningfully.
  • Earnings management around covenant thresholds.
  • Fintech lending and credit access for small firms.
  • Executive pay structure and subsequent performance.

Operations and supply chain

  • Supply chain resilience strategies after sustained disruption.
  • Whether nearshoring reduces cost or only risk.
  • Inventory strategy in food retail and waste outcomes.
  • Lean implementation in a service rather than manufacturing setting.
  • Supplier relationship length and quality outcomes.
  • Automation and workforce redeployment in warehousing.
  • Last mile delivery models and unit economics.
  • Quality management certification and actual defect rates.
  • Demand forecasting accuracy and the cost of getting it wrong.
  • Circular economy practices in a single supply chain.

Strategy, economics and entrepreneurship

  • Market entry mode choice and subsequent performance.
  • Whether acquisitions in a named sector delivered stated synergies.
  • Platform business models and competitive advantage.
  • Diversification and firm value in family owned firms.
  • The effect of a specific policy change on regional employment.
  • Minimum wage changes and small business hiring.
  • Founder characteristics and early stage survival.
  • Accelerator participation and subsequent funding.
  • Bootstrapping versus external funding on growth trajectory.
  • University spinouts and commercialisation outcomes.

Turning a topic into a supervisable question

Any item on that list is still a subject rather than a title. The narrowing process is the same one we use for every discipline.

Applied to business, the bounding step usually means naming a sector, a firm size band, a country and a period. "Hybrid working and retention" becomes "The relationship between hybrid working policy and voluntary turnover among UK professional services firms of 50 to 250 employees, 2023 to 2026". That is longer and far more answerable.

What changes for an MBA

MBA dissertation topics differ in emphasis rather than in kind. Three differences matter.

  • Practice orientation. An MBA dissertation is usually expected to produce recommendations a named organisation could act on, not only a contribution to literature.
  • Your own access is an asset. Most MBA students are working, and a single organisation study grounded in real access is stronger than a generic survey.
  • Consultancy framing is accepted. Many programmes allow a consultancy style report format, which changes the structure though not the rigour.

If you are on that route, our MBA dissertation guidance covers the format differences, and the research onion is the framework most UK business schools expect you to work through when justifying your design.

The three failure modes

  1. No access. The single biggest cause of a changed title in January. Secure your data route before you finalise anything.
  2. Too many variables. Two variables and a context is a dissertation. Five variables is a research programme.
  3. A question the literature has already settled. Spend a week reading before committing. If the answer is well established, your contribution can only be replication, which is legitimate but must be framed as such.

On the second point, the practical test is whether you can state your question in one sentence without using the word "and" more than once. If you cannot, you have more than one project.

There is a fourth failure that is less discussed. Students choose a topic because a company will sponsor or assist the research, then discover that the organisation expects a particular answer. Academic independence is assessed, and a dissertation that reads as internal advocacy will be marked down however good the access was. Agree at the outset that you will publish what you find, and say so in your ethics section.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose a business dissertation topic?

Start from the data you can realistically obtain, then pick a question that data can answer. Name your sector, firm size, country and period to bound it. Interest matters, but access is what determines whether the project is finished on time.

Which business specialism is easiest to research?

Marketing and HR are usually the most straightforward, because survey designs reach respondents who are easy to find. Finance can be the easiest of all if you are comfortable with numbers, since market and filing data is already public.

How are MBA dissertation topics different?

They are expected to produce recommendations a named organisation could act on, they benefit from the access your own employment gives you, and many programmes accept a consultancy report format. The research rigour expected is the same.

How many variables should a business dissertation have?

Two variables studied in one clear context is the right scale for a masters project. If you cannot state your question in one sentence without using "and" more than once, you probably have two projects rather than one.

What makes a business dissertation fail?

Losing data access after the title is fixed is the most common cause, followed by too many variables and by choosing a question the literature has already settled. All three are avoidable by checking before you commit.
