Getting a Management Assignment Right

Which theory answers which question, how to structure the answer, and why theory is the lens rather than the subject.

A management assignment is marked on whether you applied a theory to evidence and then critiqued the theory, not on whether you explained the theory correctly. Most students spend half their word count describing a model that the marker already knows, then run out of space for the analysis the marks are attached to.

The structure that works is short on exposition and long on application. State the organisational problem, choose one theory and justify that choice, apply it to your case, show where it fails to explain what you observe, then recommend.

Students looking for management assignment help usually want to know which theory to use. That is the right question, and it has a better answer than "the one from this week's lecture". Each model answers a specific kind of question, and choosing the one that fits your case is most of the analytical work. This guide maps the common theories to the questions they actually answer and sets out the management assignment structure UK markers expect.

One framing point before the detail. A management assignment is not a test of whether you remember a model. It is a test of whether you can use one to explain something that actually happened in an organisation, and then say honestly where the explanation runs out. Every management assignment brief we see is built around that, however it is worded.

Which theory answers which question

Two observations from marking. Maslow appears in far more assignments than it should, largely because it is memorable and taught early. At degree level it is weak analytically, because the hierarchy has limited empirical support and the model makes few testable predictions. If you use it, use it as a foil for something more defensible.

Herzberg is the better choice for most motivation questions, because the two factor distinction generates a genuine analytical move: you can classify what you observe and then ask why removing dissatisfiers did not produce motivation. Our detailed guide to Herzberg's two factor theory covers how to apply it to a case rather than describe it.

The structure that earns marks

Step four is the one that separates a 2:1 from a first. Applying a theory neatly to a case shows competence. Showing where the theory does not fit, and explaining why, shows judgement. Every model was built in a context, and naming the mismatch between that context and yours is a critical move markers explicitly look for.

A worked example of the move: "Kotter's model assumes a leadership team with the authority to create urgency. In a partnership structure where each partner holds a veto, the first step has no obvious owner, which may explain why the change stalled before step three rather than at implementation."

Word allocation for a 2,500 word management assignment

  • Introduction and problem statement: 250 words.
  • Theory selection and justification: 400 words, including why not the alternatives.
  • Application to the case: 900 words. This is the core.
  • Critique of the theory in this context: 500 words.
  • Recommendations: 300 words, specific and owned.
  • Conclusion: 150 words.

Notice that pure description of the theory has no allocation at all. Explain the model as you apply it, in the application section, rather than in a standalone block beforehand. That single change usually frees 400 words for analysis.

Topics by module

Project management

Questions usually centre on why a project overran or failed. The useful frameworks are the iron triangle for trade offs, stakeholder mapping for influence, and risk registers for what was foreseeable. The strongest answers distinguish between a project that failed and a project that was poorly specified, which are different failures with different owners.

Leadership

Transformational and transactional leadership is the standard pairing, and situational leadership is the usual third. The trap is treating transformational as automatically better. It is more effective in uncertainty and less so in stable, compliance driven settings, and saying so is the analysis.

Operations management

Lean, capacity planning, quality management and supply chain resilience. These assignments reward numbers. If you can calculate a utilisation rate or a cost of poor quality from the case data, do it, because quantified analysis is rare and stands out.

Strategic management

Porter's five forces, resource based view, PESTLE and SWOT. A warning on SWOT: it is a data gathering tool, not an analysis. A SWOT table with no interpretation is a list, and it is one of the most common causes of descriptive feedback in strategy modules.

Change management

Lewin and Kotter, usually compared. Lewin explains why change reverses, because refreezing was skipped. Kotter explains how change is sequenced. A good answer uses one as the frame and the other as the critique.

Using the case evidence properly

Most management modules give you a case study, and most students underuse it. The case is your evidence, and evidence has to be cited as specifically as a journal article would be. Quote the figure, name the paragraph, point to the exhibit. An assertion about the organisation with no anchor in the case reads as invention.

Where the case is thin on a point you need, say so explicitly rather than filling the gap with assumption. "The case does not report turnover by department, which limits how far this analysis can attribute the effect to the restructure." A marker reads that as awareness of evidence, and it is the same move a limitations section makes in a dissertation.

The three errors we correct most often

  1. Describing the theory at length. The marker knows the model. Marks are for application.
  2. Using several frameworks shallowly. One theory applied properly beats three mentioned. Multiple frameworks usually indicate uncertainty about which one fits.
  3. Recommendations that do not follow from the analysis. Every recommendation should trace back to something you established. Generic advice that could apply to any organisation reads as filler.

Frequently asked questions

How do you structure a management assignment?

State the organisational problem, choose one theory and justify it against alternatives, apply it to the evidence, critique where the theory fails to explain the case, then make specific recommendations. Explain the model as you apply it rather than in a separate descriptive block.

Which management theory should I use?

The one that answers your question. Herzberg for dissatisfaction, Kotter for how change is led, Lewin for why change reverses, Porter for why a market is difficult, Belbin for why a team misfires. Choosing to fit the case is itself part of the analysis.

Is SWOT analysis enough for a strategy assignment?

No. SWOT is a data gathering tool rather than an analysis, and a SWOT table without interpretation is one of the most common causes of descriptive feedback. Use it to organise evidence, then apply a framework that generates an argument.

How many theories should a management assignment use?

Usually one, applied properly, sometimes with a second used to critique the first. Three frameworks mentioned shallowly scores lower than one applied well, and usually signals uncertainty about which model actually fits the case.

Why is my management assignment marked as descriptive?

Most often because too much space explains the theory and too little applies it. Marks sit in the application and the critique. Cut the standalone theory section, explain the model as you use it, and spend the space on what the case shows.
