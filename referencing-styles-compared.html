Every Referencing Style, Compared

One source formatted six ways, the three families they belong to, and which discipline expects which.

Referencing styles fall into three families: author and date systems such as Harvard and APA, author and page systems such as MLA, and note or numeric systems such as Oxford, OSCOLA and Vancouver. Which one you use is decided by your department, not by preference.

The family matters more than the punctuation. Author and date keeps the evidence visible in the sentence, which suits disciplines where the currency of research matters. Note based systems move the detail to the foot of the page, which suits disciplines where the prose carries a sustained argument. Numeric systems compress citations to a number, which suits fields where sources are many and short.

Most guidance on referencing styles explains one style thoroughly and leaves you unable to switch when a module changes. Understanding the families makes every style learnable, because within a family the differences are small. This guide shows the same source in each, maps styles to disciplines, and covers the switching problems that cause most errors.

The same source, six ways

The reference list entry, by style

Harvard (Cite Them Right):
Nicol, D. (2010) 'From monologue to dialogue: improving written feedback processes in mass higher education', Assessment and Evaluation in Higher Education, 35(5), pp. 501-517.

APA 7th:
Nicol, D. (2010). From monologue to dialogue: Improving written feedback processes in mass higher education. Assessment and Evaluation in Higher Education, 35(5), 501-517.

MLA 9th:
Nicol, David. "From Monologue to Dialogue: Improving Written Feedback Processes in Mass Higher Education." Assessment and Evaluation in Higher Education, vol. 35, no. 5, 2010, pp. 501-17.

Vancouver:
1. Nicol D. From monologue to dialogue: improving written feedback processes in mass higher education. Assess Eval High Educ. 2010;35(5):501-17.

Read those four carefully and the family logic appears. Harvard and APA are nearly identical, differing mainly in punctuation and capitalisation. MLA moves the year away from the author and adds full first names. Vancouver abbreviates the journal, drops most punctuation and leads with a number.

Three differences are worth memorising because they cause the most errors when switching. APA capitalises only the first word of an article title and any proper nouns; MLA capitalises most words. APA uses an ampersand before the last author in a reference; Harvard uses "and". And Vancouver orders the reference list by order of appearance, not alphabetically, which catches out anyone moving from an author and date system.

Which style your discipline uses

These are conventions rather than rules, and your department can depart from them. Business schools occasionally require APA. Some history programmes accept Harvard. Nursing courses vary between Vancouver and Harvard depending on the university. The handbook is the authority, and it is worth checking at the start of every module rather than assuming continuity from the last one.

Oxford referencing style in the UK

Oxford is a note based system, which means a superscript number in the text and a footnote at the bottom of the page containing the full reference on first mention, then a shortened form afterwards. A bibliography usually follows at the end.

It is used mainly in history, classics, theology and philosophy, where an argument often needs a substantive aside that would break the prose if it sat in the body. That is the real advantage of note based systems: a footnote can carry discussion as well as a citation, which author and date systems cannot do gracefully.

Vancouver referencing style

Vancouver numbers sources in the order they first appear, cites them in the text as a bracketed or superscript number, and lists them numerically. It is standard in medicine, nursing and many allied health fields.

Two practical consequences. Renumbering by hand after inserting a source is close to impossible in a long document, so a reference manager is effectively required. And because a citation is just a number, the reader cannot tell how recent a source is without turning to the list, which is why Vancouver writing often names key authors in the sentence.

APA against Harvard

The question we get most often. They are the same family and differ in detail rather than approach. Harvard, in the Cite Them Right version, uses single quotation marks around article titles and "pp." before page ranges. APA uses no quotation marks around article titles, italicises the volume number, and includes a DOI where one exists.

The substantive difference is that APA is a single published standard maintained by the American Psychological Association, while Harvard is a family of institutional variations. That is why an APA question usually has one correct answer and a Harvard question often has a local one. Our full guide to Cite Them Right Harvard covers the UK standard version.

Switching styles without losing marks

  • Change the whole document, not the entries you notice. Mixed styles score worse than a consistently applied wrong one.
  • Check the ordering rule. Alphabetical for author and date and MLA, order of appearance for Vancouver.
  • Check capitalisation of titles. This is the most visible difference and the easiest to miss.
  • Re-check your reference manager output. Managers apply a style template to whatever metadata they imported, and bad metadata produces confidently wrong references.
  • Read one worked example in the new style before converting. Ten minutes here saves an hour of corrections.

The fourth point deserves emphasis. A reference manager is a formatting tool, not a verification tool. It will format a fabricated or mis-imported source as neatly as a real one, and the responsibility for accuracy remains yours. Open a sample of your final list and confirm the sources exist as described.

What is actually being assessed

Markers are not testing whether you memorised punctuation. They are testing two things: whether a reader can find every source you used, and whether you applied one system consistently. Consistency is worth more than perfection, and a reference list that is uniformly slightly wrong reads far better than one that is half right in two styles.

Beyond mechanics, your reference list is read as evidence of your reading. Its balance of recent and foundational work, of peer reviewed and grey literature, tells a marker how thoroughly you engaged with the field before any of the formatting is considered.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main referencing styles?

Harvard and APA are author and date systems, MLA is author and page, and Oxford, OSCOLA and Vancouver are note or numeric systems. Which you use is set by your department, and the family a style belongs to matters more than its punctuation.

What is the difference between APA and Harvard referencing?

They are the same family and differ in detail. APA omits quotation marks around article titles, italicises the volume number and includes a DOI. Harvard uses single quotation marks and "pp." before page ranges. APA is one published standard; Harvard is a family of local variations.

What is Oxford referencing style?

A note based system using superscript numbers in the text and footnotes carrying the full reference on first mention, with a bibliography at the end. It is used in history, classics, theology and philosophy, where footnotes can carry discussion as well as citation.

How does Vancouver referencing work?

Sources are numbered in the order they first appear, cited in the text as a number, and listed numerically rather than alphabetically. It is standard in medicine and nursing, and a reference manager is effectively required because manual renumbering is impractical.

What happens if I mix referencing styles?

Mixed styles score worse than one style applied consistently but imperfectly. Markers assess whether a reader can find every source and whether one system was used throughout, so consistency is worth more than perfect punctuation.
