How a University Report Is Laid Out

Ten sections in a fixed order, what each one is for, and why a report is not an essay with headings.

A university report is laid out in numbered sections that a reader can enter at any point: title page, executive summary, contents, introduction, method, findings, discussion, conclusions, recommendations, then references and appendices. An essay is read from start to finish. A report is designed to be skimmed by someone who needs one part of it.

That difference drives every formatting decision. Numbered headings exist so a reader can be pointed to section 4.2. The executive summary exists so a busy reader can act without reading the rest. If you understand who the report is written for, the layout stops being arbitrary.

The layout of a report is the most commonly assessed formatting skill in UK coursework, and it is one students are rarely taught directly. A report brief says "write a report" and assumes you know what that means structurally. This guide sets out the sections in order, gives you an academic report example to pattern match against, and explains what each section is actually for.

Your brief overrides everything below. Some modules drop the executive summary, some merge findings and discussion, and some specify a section numbering scheme. Read the brief twice, then use this as the default for anything it does not mention.

The sections, in order

Three of those sections cause most of the trouble, so they are worth taking slowly.

The executive summary

This is not an introduction and it is not an abstract. An introduction sets up what the report will do. An executive summary states what the report found and what it recommends, in about 150 to 250 words, so that a reader who stops there still knows the answer.

Write it last. It should contain your key findings and your main recommendations, with numbers where you have them. It should contain no background, no method and no citations. The test is simple: if someone read only your executive summary, could they take the decision the report is meant to inform?

How to write a report introduction

Four moves, in about 10 per cent of your word count. State the context briefly. State the purpose of the report, using the language of the brief. State the scope, which means what the report covers and explicitly what it does not. Then outline the structure in a sentence or two.

Scope is the part students leave out, and it is the part that protects you. "This report examines customer retention in the UK subscription market between 2023 and 2026. It does not consider acquisition costs." A marker who wanted acquisition costs now knows you made a deliberate decision rather than an omission.

Findings against discussion

Findings present the evidence without interpreting it. Discussion interprets it against the literature. Students routinely merge them, and then their findings section makes claims the evidence has not yet supported.

The practical rule is that the findings section contains no "because" and no "this suggests". It says what the data shows. The discussion then explains why, what it means and how it sits against what others have found.

Report layout against essay layout

If you have written essays for two years, a report will feel wrong at first. It is supposed to.

The biggest adjustment is bullet points. In an essay they are usually discouraged, because the connective reasoning between ideas is part of what is assessed. In a report they are expected, because the reader needs to extract items quickly. Use them for genuine lists, not as a way of avoiding writing sentences.

Academic report example: numbering in practice

1. Introduction
  1.1 Purpose and scope
  1.2 Structure of this report
2. Approach
  2.1 Data sources
  2.2 Limitations of the approach
3. Findings
  3.1 Retention by cohort
  3.2 Cancellation reasons given
4. Discussion
5. Conclusions
6. Recommendations
  6.1 Immediate actions
  6.2 Actions requiring further work

Notice that recommendations are split by urgency and that each numbered item can be referred to precisely. A reader can say "we accept 6.1 but not 6.2", which is exactly what reports exist to enable.

Figures, tables and appendices

Every figure and table is numbered and captioned, and every one is referred to in the text before it appears. A figure that is never mentioned is decoration, and markers treat it as such.

  • Tables are numbered separately from figures, and the caption sits above the table.
  • Figures include charts, diagrams and images, and the caption sits below.
  • Appendices hold the full material: the questionnaire, the raw data, the transcript. Each is lettered and each is referred to in the main text.

The appendix rule that matters is that nothing in an appendix can be load bearing. A marker is not obliged to read them. If an argument depends on something, it belongs in the body, at least in summary.

Recommendations that are actually usable

This is the section that separates a competent report from a good one. Weak recommendations are vague and unowned: "the company should improve communication". Strong ones are specific, assigned and prioritised.

A usable recommendation names the action, the owner, the timeframe and the expected effect, and it follows visibly from a finding. If a reader cannot trace a recommendation back to a numbered finding, it will read as opinion. Cross reference explicitly, so recommendation 6.1 cites finding 3.2, and the chain of reasoning is visible on the page.

A final pass before submission

  1. Update the contents page and check three entries against the real pages.
  2. Confirm every figure and table is numbered, captioned and referred to in the text.
  3. Read the executive summary alone and check it answers the brief.
  4. Check the findings section contains no interpretation.
  5. Trace each recommendation back to a numbered finding.

That takes about twenty minutes and catches the issues that cost structural marks. If your feedback has said your work describes rather than analyses, the fourth item is usually where the problem starts, and our guidance on being too descriptive and not critical enough covers the fix.

Frequently asked questions

What is the standard layout of a report?

Title page, executive summary, contents, introduction, method or approach, findings, discussion, conclusions, recommendations, then references and appendices. Sections are numbered so a reader can be directed to a specific part without reading the whole document.

What is the difference between a report and an essay?

A report uses numbered headings, an executive summary, bullet points and recommendations, and is designed to be skimmed by section. An essay is read from start to finish, usually without headings, and the connective reasoning between ideas is itself assessed.

How do you write a report introduction?

State the context briefly, state the purpose using the language of the brief, state the scope including what the report does not cover, then outline the structure. Scope is the part most often left out and the part that protects you from a marker's expectations.

What goes in an executive summary?

Your key findings and main recommendations in about 150 to 250 words, with numbers where you have them. No background, no method, no citations. Write it last, and test it by asking whether a reader could act on it without reading further.

Should findings and discussion be separate sections?

Usually yes. Findings present the evidence without interpreting it, so no "because" and no "this suggests". Discussion then interprets that evidence against the literature. Merging them tends to produce claims the evidence has not yet supported.
