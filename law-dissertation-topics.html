Choosing a Law Dissertation Topic

Forty supervisable ideas by area, and the four tests that separate a title from a subject.

A good law dissertation topic is a narrow legal question where the law is genuinely unsettled and the sources you need are ones you can actually reach. "Human rights" is a subject. "Whether the Article 8 proportionality test has been applied consistently to automated data processing since 2020" is a law dissertation topic.

The most common mistake is choosing something too broad and interesting rather than something narrow and arguable. Breadth cannot be rescued later. A tightly bounded question that you can answer from reported cases will always mark higher than a sweeping one you can only summarise.

Choosing from a list of law dissertation topics is the easy part. Turning one into a supervisable title is where most of the work sits, and it is where a law dissertation is won or lost. This guide gives you forty starting points grouped by area, then shows you the narrowing process we take students through. If you are still deciding which area to commit to, start from the modules where you already have the doctrinal footing, because a law dissertation runs on the case law you can read fluently.

Which areas are actually supervisable

Not all areas of law are equally practical for an undergraduate dissertation. The constraint is rarely interest. It is whether there is a settled enough body of primary source material for you to analyse in the time you have.

Comparative topics attract students and defeat them more often than any other category. Comparing two jurisdictions means reading two bodies of law properly, often one of them in translation, and undergraduate timetables rarely allow it. If you want a comparative flavour, use a single jurisdiction as your subject and the other as a brief point of contrast in the discussion.

Forty law dissertation ideas by area

Criminal law and criminology

  • Consistency in sentencing for the same offence across different Crown Court regions.
  • The continued use of joint enterprise after the Supreme Court's correction of the law.
  • Whether the reasonable person standard in self defence accommodates domestic abuse survivors.
  • Diversion schemes for young offenders and the evidence on reoffending.
  • Criminalisation of coercive control, and what the reported prosecutions show.
  • The evidential treatment of digital material in sexual offence trials.
  • Whether sentencing guidelines have narrowed judicial discretion too far.
  • Corporate criminal liability and the identification doctrine.

Human rights and public law

  • Proportionality under Article 8 as applied to automated decision making.
  • The margin of appreciation in religious dress cases.
  • Judicial review standing rules and access to environmental justice.
  • Whether deprivation of citizenship powers are compatible with Article 8.
  • Article 10 and the regulation of online speech.
  • The duty of candour in public authority litigation.
  • Prisoners' rights and the limits of the least eligibility principle.
  • Positive obligations under Article 2 in healthcare settings.

Commercial and company law

  • Directors' duties under section 172 and whether stakeholder wording changes decisions.
  • Piercing the corporate veil after the Supreme Court restated the test.
  • Shareholder remedies for unfair prejudice in small private companies.
  • Good faith in commercial contracts, and whether an implied duty is emerging.
  • Penalty clauses and the legitimate interest test in practice.
  • Insolvency priority rules and their effect on unsecured trade creditors.
  • Whether ESG disclosure obligations have changed board behaviour.
  • Force majeure drafting after a decade of disruption litigation.

Family, medical and environmental

  • The welfare checklist and the presumption of parental involvement.
  • Financial remedy discretion and the case for a statutory formula.
  • Consent after Montgomery and what it requires of clinicians in practice.
  • Best interests decisions for patients without capacity.
  • Withdrawal of life sustaining treatment and the role of the court.
  • Climate litigation against private companies and the duty of care question.
  • Environmental permitting enforcement and whether penalties deter.
  • Whether the public trust doctrine has any purchase in English law.

Technology, employment and property

  • Employment status for platform workers after the Supreme Court's ruling.
  • Algorithmic management and the implied duty of trust and confidence.
  • Data protection enforcement and whether fines change corporate practice.
  • Liability for harm caused by autonomous systems.
  • Leasehold reform and the fairness of ground rent.
  • Adverse possession and the registered land regime.
  • Whether the law of nuisance can address noise from short term lets.
  • Restrictive covenants and modern development pressure.

The four tests a title must pass

Take any of those legal thesis topics and run it through this sequence. A title that survives all four is one a supervisor will approve.

Is there a genuine tension? A dissertation needs something to argue. If every court and commentator agrees, you have a summary rather than a thesis. Look for a dissent, a line of cases that pull in different directions, or a statutory provision that judges have read inconsistently.

Is it bounded? One jurisdiction, one period, one class of case. "Since 2015 in England and Wales" is a boundary. "In modern society" is not.

Can you name the sources? Before you commit, list the six to ten primary sources you will analyse. If you cannot name them, the topic is not yet real.

Is it answerable in the word count? An undergraduate law dissertation is usually 8,000 to 12,000 words. That is roughly one substantial argument with three supporting strands.

Criminology topics are not law topics

Worth separating, because students conflate them and supervisors do not. Criminology dissertation topics are empirical and social scientific: they ask what happens, to whom and why, and they are answered with data. Law topics are doctrinal: they ask what the law is or ought to be, and they are answered with cases and statute.

A criminology dissertation on sentencing disparity collects and analyses sentencing data. A law dissertation on the same subject analyses the guidelines, the appellate authority and the reasoning. If your programme is straight law, the doctrinal version is almost always the safer choice, because it uses the research skills your degree actually taught you.

If you do take an empirical route, the design has to be defensible, and our guidance on justifying your research methodology covers what a supervisor will ask for.

What markers reward in a law dissertation

  • Primary sources read directly. Cases and statute first, textbooks second. A dissertation built on secondary commentary reads as second hand.
  • A position that is defended. Setting out both sides and declining to choose is the commonest route to a mid range mark.
  • Correct OSCOLA footnoting. Law in the UK uses OSCOLA rather than Harvard, and consistency is assessed.
  • Engagement with the counter-argument. The strongest chapters state the best version of the opposing case before answering it.

The second point is the one most worth internalising. If your feedback has repeatedly said your work is descriptive, the fix is structural rather than stylistic, and we set it out in our guidance on being too descriptive and not critical enough.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose a law dissertation topic?

Start from a module you did well in, find a point where the law is genuinely unsettled, then bound it to one jurisdiction and one period. Before committing, list the six to ten primary sources you will analyse. If you cannot name them, the topic is not ready.

How long is an undergraduate law dissertation?

Usually 8,000 to 12,000 words in the UK, though your programme handbook is the only figure that matters. That length supports roughly one substantial argument with three supporting strands, which is why narrowing the question early is so important.

What is the difference between law and criminology dissertation topics?

Law topics are doctrinal and ask what the law is or should be, answered through cases and statute. Criminology topics are empirical and ask what happens and why, answered with data. Straight law programmes usually expect the doctrinal version.

Should a law dissertation use Harvard referencing?

No. UK law uses OSCOLA, which is a footnote system rather than an author and date one. Check your handbook, because a small number of socio-legal programmes accept Harvard, but OSCOLA is the default and consistency is marked.

Are comparative law dissertation topics a good idea?

Rarely at undergraduate level. Comparing two jurisdictions means reading two bodies of law properly, often one in translation, which most timetables do not allow. Use one jurisdiction as your subject and the other as a brief point of contrast instead.
