Writing a Bibliography

What belongs in it, how it is ordered, and why it is not the same thing as a reference list.

A bibliography lists every source you consulted while preparing your work, whether or not you cited it, arranged alphabetically by author surname. A reference list contains only the sources you actually cited. They are different documents and submitting the wrong one is a common, avoidable deduction.

Unless your handbook explicitly asks for a bibliography, a UK submission almost always wants a reference list. Where a bibliography is requested, it is usually in history, classics or a module that wants evidence of reading breadth.

Knowing how to write a bibliography is mostly about knowing which document you have been asked for. The mechanics are straightforward once that is settled: same entry format as your referencing style, alphabetical order, no numbering. This guide covers the format, the ordering rules that get fiddly, and the annotated variant that some modules require.

Bibliography or reference list?

The distinction exists because the two documents answer different questions. A reference list answers "where did these specific claims come from?", and every entry is verifiable against a citation in your text. A bibliography answers "what did you read?", and it demonstrates the breadth of your engagement with a field.

That is why bibliographies survive in disciplines where wide reading is itself part of the assessment, and why they have largely disappeared from disciplines where the link between claim and evidence is what matters. Note that a few departments ask for both, with the reference list first.

Bibliography format

Entries use exactly the same format as your referencing style. A Harvard bibliography entry is identical to a Harvard reference list entry. Nothing about the punctuation changes; only what qualifies for inclusion does.

Bibliography example, Harvard

Bryman, A. (2016) Social research methods. 5th edn. Oxford: Oxford University Press.

Department for Education (2025) Schools, pupils and their characteristics. Available at: https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics (Accessed: 20 October 2026).

Nicol, D. (2010) 'From monologue to dialogue: improving written feedback processes in mass higher education', Assessment and Evaluation in Higher Education, 35(5), pp. 501-517.

Pears, R. and Shields, G. (2022) Cite them right: the essential referencing guide. 12th edn. London: Bloomsbury Academic.

Silverman, D. (2020) 'Doing qualitative research', in Flick, U. (ed.) The SAGE handbook of qualitative data collection. London: SAGE, pp. 45-62.

Note three things about that list. It is a single sequence rather than being split into books, articles and websites. It is not numbered or bulleted. And the organisation appears in the alphabetical run at D for Department, because where there is no individual author the organisation takes that position.

The ordering rules that get fiddly

  • Same author, several works: order by year, earliest first.
  • Same author, same year: add lowercase letters ordered by title, so 2024a then 2024b, and use the same letters in any citations.
  • Single author then co-authored: the single authored works come first, then the co-authored ones alphabetically by second author.
  • Surnames with prefixes: follow the convention of the person's own language. Dutch "van" is usually filed under the main name, while "De" in some traditions is filed under D. If unsure, be consistent.
  • No author at all: file under the first significant word of the title, ignoring "A" and "The".
  • Numbers at the start of a title: file as if the number were spelled out.

Most reference managers handle the first three correctly and the rest inconsistently. Check the awkward cases by eye before submitting, because they cluster at the top and bottom of the list where they are most visible.

The annotated variant

Some modules ask for an annotated bibliography, which adds a short paragraph after each entry summarising the source and evaluating its usefulness for your work. The annotation is typically 100 to 150 words and does two jobs: it shows you read the source, and it shows you can judge it.

A weak annotation summarises. A strong one evaluates: what the source argues, how robust its evidence is, and what it contributes to your specific question. We cover the full format, including worked annotations, in our guide to writing an annotated bibliography.

What markers actually read in a bibliography

Formatting is checked quickly. What a marker reads for takes longer and matters more.

  1. Currency. A bibliography on a contemporary topic with nothing after 2018 signals reading that stopped early.
  2. Source quality. The balance of peer reviewed journals against textbooks, websites and grey literature. A list dominated by websites reads as under-researched regardless of topic.
  3. Foundational work. Every field has works that a competent student would have met. Their absence is noticed.
  4. Breadth against your argument. A bibliography containing only sources that agree with your position suggests you read to confirm rather than to understand.

That last point is worth acting on. If your list contains nothing that challenges your conclusion, go and find the strongest opposing source before you submit. It will improve the bibliography and, more usefully, the argument.

Building it as you go, not at the end

The single practical habit that makes bibliographies painless is recording sources at the moment you read them rather than reconstructing the list the night before submission. Reconstruction is where fabricated and half remembered entries come from, because by then you are working from notes rather than from the source.

Whatever tool you use, capture four things when you first open a source: the full author list, the exact title, the year, and where you found it. Those four make the entry recoverable in any style later. Students who adopt this stop losing marks on referencing within about two assignments, because the task becomes a five minute export rather than an evening of detective work.

Check the entries are real

A newer risk worth naming. Reference generators and AI tools both produce entries that are perfectly formatted for sources that do not exist. A fabricated entry is treated as an integrity matter rather than a formatting one, because it claims reading that did not happen.

Before submitting, open a sample of your list and confirm each is real, by the authors named, in the year given. If a tool built the list, check every entry. Our guidance on what an AI flag means explains how this is reviewed and what evidence helps.

Frequently asked questions

How do you write a bibliography?

Use the same entry format as your referencing style, list every source you consulted whether cited or not, and arrange it as a single alphabetical sequence by author surname. Do not number it and do not split it into sections by source type.

What is the difference between a bibliography and a reference list?

A reference list contains only the sources you cited in the text. A bibliography contains everything you consulted. Unless your handbook asks for a bibliography, a UK submission almost always wants a reference list.

Is a bibliography in alphabetical order?

Yes, by author surname, as one sequence. Works by the same author are ordered by year, and works by the same author in the same year take lowercase letters ordered by title, such as 2024a and 2024b.

Should a bibliography be split into books, articles and websites?

No. It is a single alphabetical sequence regardless of source type. Splitting by type is a common error that makes entries harder to find, which defeats the purpose of the list.

What is an annotated bibliography?

A bibliography where each entry is followed by 100 to 150 words summarising and evaluating the source. A strong annotation judges the quality of the evidence and its relevance to your question rather than only summarising the content.
