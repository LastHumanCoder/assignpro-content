Academic support

What Legitimate Support Looks Like

Tutoring, feedback and proofreading explained, with the line UK universities actually draw.

Legitimate assignment writing help explains concepts, reviews your own draft and gives you feedback to act on. The student researches, writes and submits the work. That is the line every UK university draws, and it is the line we work strictly inside.

Anything that produces text for you to submit as your own is contract cheating. It is an academic offence, it is now illegal to provide in England under the Skills and Post-16 Education Act 2022, and no marking benefit is worth the risk to your degree. If a service offers to produce your work, that is the thing to walk away from.

Searching for assignment writing help turns up a confusing mix of tutoring services, assignment editing services and things that are neither. The word "help" is doing a lot of unspoken work in those listings, and an assignment support service and an assignment production service can look identical from a search result. This guide sets out what each kind of support actually does, what you get back from it, and how to tell a legitimate service from one that will put your degree at risk. We are a tutoring service, so we have an interest here, and the most useful thing we can do is be precise about where the boundary sits.

The four kinds of support

Notice what every row has in common. In each case the output is something you then act on: an explanation you understood, comments you respond to, corrections you apply. None of them produce text that goes into your submission unchanged, because that is the boundary.

Tutoring

A tutor explains a concept, a method or a marking criterion until you can use it. This is the highest value form of support because what you gain transfers to the next assignment and the one after. It is also the slowest, which is why students under deadline pressure look for shortcuts.

Feedback on a draft

You write a draft, a subject specialist reads it against the marking criteria, and you get comments identifying where the argument is thin, where evidence is missing and where structure fights the reader. You then revise it yourself. This is what a supervisor does, and getting it earlier than your supervisor can is the legitimate advantage.

Proofreading

An academic proofreading service marks surface errors: spelling, punctuation, grammar, consistency of referencing format. A proofreader does not rewrite your sentences, does not restructure your argument and does not add content. UK universities generally permit proofreading and many publish a policy defining exactly what is allowed.

The distinction that matters is between marking an error and fixing it in a way that changes your voice. "This sentence has no main verb" is proofreading. Replacing your paragraph with a better written one is not.

Structure and referencing review

Someone reads your outline, or your reference list, and tells you where it departs from what the brief or the style guide requires. You make the changes. This is the cheapest and one of the most effective interventions, because structural problems cost more marks than sentence level ones.

How a feedback cycle should work

The test of good support is what happens next time. If you needed the same help on the following assignment, the support did not work. Our own measure is whether a student stops needing us for a particular skill, which is an awkward business model and the correct educational one.

Have a draft and a marking rubric? Send both through the academic guidance form. A subject tutor will map your draft against the criteria and show you exactly where the marks are currently missing, so you can revise it yourself before the deadline.

How to tell a legitimate service apart

  • Read what they promise. A service offering to produce work for submission is offering contract cheating whatever it calls itself. A service offering explanation, feedback and review is not.
  • Look for a stated policy on academic integrity. Legitimate providers say plainly that the student submits their own work.
  • Be wary of guaranteed grades. Nobody can guarantee a mark awarded by your department, and the promise implies something other than tutoring.
  • Check whether they ask for your brief and your draft. Tutoring needs your work to respond to. A service that only needs the title is not planning to give feedback.
  • Ask who writes the guidance. Human written, subject specialist guidance is the point. Generated material is both less useful and more likely to introduce errors.

The AI question

Generative tools have made this boundary sharper rather than blurrier. Most UK universities now treat submitting AI generated text as your own as an integrity offence, in the same category as contract cheating.

Two practical points. First, using such a tool to explain a concept to you is usually permitted, in the same way a textbook is, while using it to produce your prose usually is not. Your department's policy is the authority and they vary more than students assume. Second, detection is imperfect in both directions, which means honest students are sometimes flagged. If that happens, the evidence that helps is your process: version history, notes, drafts and reading. Our guidance on responding to an AI flag sets out what to gather and how such reviews work.

All of our own guidance is human written by subject specialist tutors and is AI free, which matters both for quality and because material handed to you by a generator is exactly what you should not be putting in front of a marker.

Why the deadline is the real risk

Almost nobody sets out to commission work. What happens is that a deadline arrives with the assignment barely started, and a service promising a finished document looks like the only exit. The decision is made under pressure, in a few hours, and it is irreversible in a way the missed deadline would not have been.

Worth knowing: most UK universities have an extenuating circumstances or mitigating circumstances process, and a late penalty is usually a fixed deduction rather than a zero. A capped mark on a late submission is recoverable. An integrity finding sits on your record and can end a degree. If you are at that point, talk to your personal tutor or student union before you talk to anyone selling a document.

Getting the most from support

  1. Come with the brief and the criteria. Feedback without the rubric is opinion.
  2. Come earlier than feels necessary. Structural advice is worth most before you have written to a structure.
  3. Bring a specific question. "Is this any good?" produces vague answers. "Does section three actually evaluate, or only describe?" produces useful ones.
  4. Act on the feedback yourself. This is the part that produces the learning, and it is the part that keeps the work yours.
  5. Keep the pattern. Write down the correction so the same error does not recur.

Frequently asked questions

Is assignment writing help allowed at UK universities?

Tutoring, feedback on your own draft and proofreading are generally permitted, and many universities publish a policy defining what proofreading may cover. Having work produced for you to submit is contract cheating, which is an academic offence and illegal to provide in England.

What can an academic proofreading service change?

Surface errors only: spelling, punctuation, grammar and consistency of referencing format. A proofreader marks errors rather than rewriting sentences, restructuring arguments or adding content, because those change the work from yours to somebody else's.

How do I tell a legitimate service from contract cheating?

Read what is promised. Explanation, feedback and review are legitimate; producing work for submission is not. Legitimate services state an academic integrity policy, ask to see your brief and your draft, and do not guarantee grades.

Can I use AI tools for my assignment?

Your department's policy decides, and policies vary more than students assume. Using a tool to explain a concept is usually permitted; submitting generated text as your own is usually an integrity offence. Keep drafts and version history as evidence of your process.

What should I bring to a tutoring session?

The brief, the marking criteria and your draft, plus one specific question. Feedback without the rubric is opinion, and a precise question such as "does section three evaluate or only describe?" produces far more useful guidance than asking whether the work is good.
