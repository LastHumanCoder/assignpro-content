How Long a PhD Thesis Should Be

Typical ranges by discipline, how the words divide across chapters, and what actually counts.

A UK PhD thesis is usually between 40,000 and 100,000 words, depending on discipline. Humanities theses sit at the top of that range, sciences and engineering at the bottom, and social sciences in between at roughly 70,000 to 90,000.

The figure that matters is not any of those. It is the maximum in your own institution's research degree regulations, which is a hard limit and is usually expressed as an upper bound rather than a target. There is no minimum in most regulations, and no examiner has ever passed a thesis for being long enough.

Students ask how many words is a phd thesis far more often than the question deserves, usually because the number feels like the shape of the task. It is not. A phd thesis is examined on whether it makes an original contribution and defends it, and the length follows from what that takes. Still, the ranges are useful for planning, so here they are with the reasoning behind why they differ.

Ranges by discipline

The variation is not about rigour. It is about where the argument lives. In history or philosophy, the reasoning is carried entirely in prose, so the prose has to be long enough to carry it. In experimental sciences, a great deal of the contribution sits in figures, tables and supplementary data that do not count towards the total. A 45,000 word chemistry thesis and a 95,000 word history thesis can represent the same three years of work.

Two practical consequences. First, never compare your word count with someone in another department, because you will either panic or relax for no reason. Second, if your supervisor says your draft is too long, that is a comment about redundancy rather than about the number.

How the words divide

Literature review and findings take half the thesis between them. The introduction and conclusion together take about 15 per cent, which is more than most students allow and is worth protecting, because those two chapters are what an examiner reads most carefully.

That split is a starting point rather than a rule. A thesis with three empirical studies will have three findings chapters and a shorter literature review. A theoretical thesis may have no findings chapter at all. What holds across shapes is that the methodology chapter is rarely the longest, and that a literature review consuming 40 per cent of the thesis usually signals a contribution that has not yet been identified.

What counts towards the limit

Regulations differ, so check yours, but the usual UK pattern is:

  • Counted: the main body from introduction to conclusion, including footnotes in most institutions.
  • Not counted: references and bibliography, appendices, tables, figure captions, the contents page and front matter.
  • Varies: footnotes. Some humanities departments count them, which matters a great deal when your argument runs partly in the notes.

The appendix exclusion is the one students try to exploit, and examiners notice. Moving a chapter's worth of argument into an appendix to meet a limit does not work, because nothing load bearing may sit in an appendix. If the examiner has to read it to follow your argument, it belongs in the body.

When you are too long

Being over is far more common than being under. The cuts that hurt least, in order:

  1. Literature that does not earn its place. Every source should be doing argumentative work. Sources included to demonstrate reading can go.
  2. Repeated framing. Most theses re-explain their theoretical framework at the start of each chapter. Say it once, properly, and cross reference.
  3. Method description that belongs in an appendix. The full instrument goes in the appendix; the justification stays in the body.
  4. Findings reported twice. A table and then a paragraph restating the table. Keep the table and interpret it instead.
  5. Hedging. "It could perhaps be tentatively suggested that" is seven words doing the work of one.

The second of those usually recovers several thousand words on its own, and it improves the thesis, because repetition reads as uncertainty about whether the reader was paying attention.

When you are short

A short draft is almost never a word count problem. It is usually one of three things: the literature review has not engaged with the debate, the discussion has not connected findings back to that literature, or the contribution has not been stated explicitly enough to be defended.

Adding words to a thesis that is short for those reasons makes it worse. The fix is to write the missing analysis, and the length follows. If your discussion chapter is thin, our guidance on moving from description to critical analysis covers the habit that usually causes it.

The word count and the viva

One reason the number matters less than students expect is that the examination is oral. Your examiners will have read the thesis, but the defence turns on whether you can state your contribution in a sentence and defend the choices behind it. A long thesis does not help you there, and a bloated literature review actively hurts, because it gives examiners more surface to question without adding to what you are defending.

A useful exercise at around the 70 per cent mark is to write your contribution as three bullet points and then check that each one is visible in the chapter structure. If a claim you would make in the viva has no chapter behind it, that is where the remaining words should go. If a chapter supports no claim you would make in the viva, that is where the cuts are.

A planning method that works

Take your upper limit, subtract 10 per cent as a buffer, then allocate by the percentages above. Write those allocations into your chapter outline before you draft anything.

Then treat each allocation as a budget rather than a target. A chapter that comes in under budget is not a problem; it frees space for one that needs more. What the budget prevents is the common failure where the literature review reaches 35,000 words before the writer notices, and the discussion is then written in three weeks.

Review the allocations at each supervision. A thesis whose shape changes is normal. A thesis whose shape changes without anyone noticing is how people end up rewriting in the final year.

Frequently asked questions

How many words is a PhD thesis?

Usually 40,000 to 100,000 words in the UK, depending on discipline. Humanities sit at the top of that range, sciences and engineering at the bottom, and social sciences around 70,000 to 90,000. Your institution's regulations set the binding limit.

Is there a minimum word count for a PhD?

Most UK regulations set a maximum rather than a minimum. A thesis is examined on whether it makes and defends an original contribution, and no examiner passes a thesis for reaching a length. A short draft usually signals missing analysis rather than missing words.

Do references and appendices count towards the word limit?

Usually not. The main body from introduction to conclusion is counted, while references, bibliography, appendices, tables and front matter are typically excluded. Footnotes vary by institution, which matters most in humanities disciplines.

How should a thesis word count be divided across chapters?

A common split is 8 per cent introduction, 25 per cent literature review, 15 per cent methodology, 25 per cent findings, 20 per cent discussion and 7 per cent conclusion. Treat it as a budget to review at each supervision rather than a fixed rule.

What should I cut if my thesis is too long?

Start with literature that is not doing argumentative work, then repeated framing at the start of each chapter, then method detail that belongs in an appendix. Removing repeated framing alone often recovers several thousand words and improves the thesis.
