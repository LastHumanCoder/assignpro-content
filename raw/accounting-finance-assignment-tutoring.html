Internal sources of finance: a dedicated look

Coursework and case studies often ask you to recommend how a business should fund a project, and a strong answer starts by weighing internal sources of finance before reaching for external borrowing. Internal finance is capital the business generates itself, so it carries no interest cost and no dilution of ownership. We tutor students to explain each source, its limits and when it is appropriate.

Retained profit

Earnings kept in the business rather than paid as dividends. Cheap and flexible, but limited by past performance and shareholder expectations.

Sale of assets

Releasing cash from underused or surplus assets. Useful for a one-off need, but you cannot repeat it indefinitely.

Working capital management

Freeing cash by tightening receivables, managing payables and reducing excess inventory. Low cost, with an operational ceiling.

Sale and leaseback

Selling an asset then leasing it back to release capital while retaining use. Sits at the boundary of internal and external finance.

Ratio analysis, a small illustrative example

Suppose a company reports the figures below for one year. We do not fill in the answer for you; we work the first ratio with you, then coach you to complete and interpret the rest.

Current assets
£420,000
Current liabilities
£300,000
Inventory
£150,000

Current ratio = 420,000 / 300,000 = 1.40. Quick ratio = (420,000 - 150,000) / 300,000 = 0.90. The coaching point is not the arithmetic; it is the interpretation. A current ratio of 1.40 looks healthy, but a quick ratio below 1.0 flags reliance on inventory to meet short-term obligations. Your job in the assignment is to explain that tension. We help you get there.
