Referencing Any Source in Harvard

One pattern, six source types, and the secondary referencing rule that trips up most first years.

To reference a book in Harvard, give the author's surname and initial, the year in brackets, the title in italics, the edition if it is not the first, then the place of publication and the publisher. So: Pears, R. and Shields, G. (2022) Cite them right: the essential referencing guide. 12th edn. London: Bloomsbury Academic.

Every other source type in Harvard keeps that same opening, author and year, and changes only what comes after the title. Learn the spine once and unfamiliar sources stop being a problem.

Most guidance on how to reference a book harvard style gives you one example and leaves you stuck the moment you hit a YouTube video or a lecture slide. This piece covers the six source types UK students actually cite, in Harvard, with the awkward cases included. We tutor referencing every week, so these are the patterns that come up rather than the exhaustive list you would find in the full guide.

One thing to settle before the examples. Harvard is a family of styles rather than a single governed one, and most UK universities follow the Cite Them Right version. If your handbook shows something slightly different, follow the handbook. Our fuller explanation of Cite Them Right Harvard covers why the variations exist.

The pattern behind every Harvard reference

Author, year, what it is called, then where to find it. That is the whole logic. What changes between source types is only the final part, because a book is found by publisher and a web page is found by URL.

Notice that the title is italicised when it is a whole, standalone work and placed in single quotation marks when it is a part of something larger. A book title is italicised. A chapter title is quoted and the book it sits in is italicised. An article title is quoted and the journal is italicised. That one distinction resolves a large share of formatting errors.

Worked examples by source type

Book, one author

Bryman, A. (2016) Social research methods. 5th edn. Oxford: Oxford University Press.

Book, four or more authors

Give every author in the reference list. In the text, use the first surname then et al.

Chapter in an edited book

Silverman, D. (2020) 'Doing qualitative research', in Flick, U. (ed.) The SAGE handbook of qualitative data collection. London: SAGE, pp. 45-62.

Journal article

Nicol, D. (2010) 'From monologue to dialogue: improving written feedback processes in mass higher education', Assessment and Evaluation in Higher Education, 35(5), pp. 501-517.

Website

Office for National Statistics (2024) Labour market overview. Available at: https://www.ons.gov.uk/employmentandlabourmarket (Accessed: 9 October 2026).

YouTube video

Royal Society (2023) How vaccines work. Available at: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=example (Accessed: 9 October 2026).

Lecture or module material

Ahmed, S. (2026) 'Research design and sampling'. BUS3021: Research Methods. University of Manchester. 14 February.

How to reference a website in Harvard

Two elements do the work. "Available at:" followed by the full URL, and "(Accessed: date)" with the date you last opened it. Both are required, because web pages change and the access date is what pins your claim to a moment.

Where there is no individual author, the organisation is the author. Where there is no date, write "no date" rather than guessing or using the access year. And reference the specific page you used, not the site's home page, because a marker should be able to follow your reference to the exact claim.

How to reference a YouTube video in Harvard

The author is whoever uploaded it, which is usually a channel name rather than a person. The year is the year of upload. The video title goes in italics because the video is a standalone work. Then the availability statement, exactly as for a web page.

A word of caution that has nothing to do with formatting. A YouTube video is rarely a strong academic source. It is appropriate when the video is itself the object of study, or when it is a recorded lecture or a primary document. It is not a substitute for a peer reviewed article, and a reference list weighted towards video will affect your mark regardless of how well it is formatted.

How to reference a lecture in Harvard

Lecturer surname and initial, year, lecture title in single quotation marks, module code and title, institution, and the date delivered. Check your handbook first, because some departments discourage citing lectures altogether on the grounds that they are not publicly available. Where that is the rule, use the sources the lecture itself cited, which is better practice anyway.

Secondary referencing in Harvard

This is the rule students get wrong most often, and it matters because getting it wrong means claiming to have read something you have not.

The principle is simple. You reference what you actually read. If you read Pears, and Pears quotes Kolb, then your in text citation acknowledges both and your reference list contains only Pears.

Markers dislike heavy secondary referencing, and reasonably so. Each layer adds a chance that the original meaning has drifted. If a source matters to your argument, find it and read it. Secondary referencing is for when the original is genuinely unobtainable, out of print, in a language you do not read, or in an archive you cannot reach.

Awkward cases, answered quickly

  • No author. Use the organisation. If there is genuinely none, begin with the title.
  • Two works, same author, same year. Add lowercase letters, 2024a and 2024b, ordered by title, and use the same letters in the text.
  • An ebook read on a device. Reference it as the print book if the edition details match. Only note the platform where pagination differs.
  • A PDF report. Treat it as a web source, with the organisation as author and an availability statement.
  • A translated work. Give the author, the year of the translation you read, and note the translator.
  • Legislation or cases. These follow legal citation conventions rather than Harvard. Law students should use OSCOLA.

Check your references exist

A newer problem worth naming. Reference generators and AI tools both produce entries that look perfectly formatted for sources that do not exist. A fabricated reference is treated far more seriously than a badly punctuated one, because it goes to academic integrity rather than to formatting.

The check is cheap. Before you submit, open three references from your list at random and confirm they are real, by the authors named, in the year given. Frequently asked questions

How do you reference a book in Harvard?

Author surname and initial, year in brackets, title in italics, edition if not the first, then place of publication and publisher. For example: Bryman, A. (2016) Social research methods. 5th edn. Oxford: Oxford University Press.

How do you reference a website in Harvard?

Author or organisation, year, page title in italics, then "Available at:" with the full URL and "(Accessed: date)". Reference the specific page rather than the home page, and write "no date" if the page shows no publication year.

How do you reference a YouTube video in Harvard?

The uploader is the author and is often a channel name. Give the year of upload, the video title in italics, then "Available at:" with the URL and the access date. Consider whether a video is an appropriate academic source before citing it.

What is secondary referencing in Harvard?

Citing a source you found quoted inside another source. Write it as (Kolb, 1984, cited in Pears, 2022) in the text, and put only Pears in your reference list, because Pears is what you actually read. Find and read the original wherever you can.

Can you reference a lecture in Harvard?

Yes, using the lecturer's name, year, lecture title in quotation marks, module code, institution and date. Check your handbook first, because some departments discourage citing lectures since they are not publicly available.
